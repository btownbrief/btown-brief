This is the Btown Brief for Monday, November 23rd.

If you've gotten used to leaving the coat at home this November, that streak just ended. The cold front came through midday, and this morning's mid 50s are sliding back through the 40s this evening on a stiff northwest wind, with clouds filling back in tonight and a low around 31. Tuesday is the real shock to the system, mostly sunny but brisk with a high only in the upper 30s, so dig out the warm layers, and Tuesday night drops into the low 20s. Wednesday is about as good a travel day as late November gets, sunny and calm with a high in the upper 30s. Thanksgiving starts dry with a high in the low 40s, but rain chances creep in by late afternoon and a cold rain settles in Thursday night and keeps going into Black Friday, with any sleet or freezing rain at the start mostly up north, so give yourself extra time if you're driving home Thursday night. For patio, swim, and sunset scores as the week goes on, keep Burlington Right Now handy.

[pause] At Hack Club in Burlington, Teens 'Make Cool Stuff' Using Big Tech's Money.

"Some of the best up-and-coming programmers in the world are right there in Burlington," Clay Nicholson said, per Seven Days.

Hack Club, a free nonprofit that teaches 13 to 18 year olds to code, build electronics and use AI, is headquartered in an old icehouse on Burlington's waterfront, where teen fellows who moved here from 15 countries spend a gap year dreaming up challenges for its more than 186,000 members. Nicholson, of Williston, never took a computer science class in high school and is now at MIT, while his alma mater, CVU, eliminated its computer science program this year due to budget cuts. Last year Hack Club spent $6.3 million educating more than 100,000 teens, backed by tech titans like Elon Musk, who has given $10 million since 2020. Cofounder Christina Asquith says it neither solicits nor accepts money from AI labs, surveillance companies or military hardware makers, adding that Hack Club "is not for sale."

[pause] For 100 years, a nonprofit has provided free services for visually impaired Vermonters. Now it's looking to scale up.

"Whatever their challenge might be with their visual impairment, we help them to reach the goals that they want to strive for," Daniel Norris said, per VTDigger.

The Vermont Association for the Blind and Visually Impaired marked its centennial last Tuesday at its Kimball Street office in South Burlington, where 6 year old Milo Carr buried the key to a time capsule in a patch slated to become a sensory garden. It helps about 1,500 Vermonters a year with everything from cooking to braille to finding a guide dog, but Norris, its executive director, says that's only 10% of the state's 15,000 significantly visually impaired people, a number he expects to be closer to 25,000 by 2030. As the state ages, it has launched a capital campaign to expand its endowment and meet needs "from the cradle to the cane." Blind teacher Anne Connelly reorganized the group in 1926 and brought Vermont's first guide dog to Burlington in 1930, a walk the city recreated last month.

[pause] Burlington leaders, businesses discuss downtown challenges ahead of holidays.

"We’re making headway, but this is a very stressful time for the city," Ernie Pomerleau said, per WCAX.

Mayor Emma Mulvaney-Stanak introduced new police chief Anthony Geraci at Friday's meeting with downtown businesses and laid out 100 events over the winter, ongoing foot patrols and a goal of 1,200 housing units by the end of 2028. With no space or staff found for a large shelter, the city will only have pop up shelters that open at 10 degrees below zero, and some continue to call for more accountability for people who cause disruptions or refuse help. The planned overdose prevention center split the room again.

[pause] Fired Shelburne police sergeant stripped of police certification for role in deadly crash.

"I want everybody to know he had a family," Donna Hayes said, per VTDigger.

The Vermont Criminal Justice Council voted last week to revoke Kyle Kapitanski's law enforcement certification, with the option to recertify at its discretion after a year. Kapitanski was driving 40 to 45 mph in a 35 mph zone on Shelburne Road in South Burlington when he struck and killed 38 year old Sean Hayes of Burlington in November 2024, and the charging affidavit says he had YouTube videos playing on a tablet mounted in his cruiser in the lead up to the crash. Hayes' mother told the council Kapitanski should never be certified in law enforcement again.

[pause] Bar Renée Staff Makes Wine From an Abandoned Vineyard.

"I’ve made wine my whole life but never with these grapes," Joe Pedicini said, per Seven Days.

The crew at Bar Renée on Main Street spent the season reviving a tiny abandoned vineyard behind a farmhouse in Grand Isle after its new owners offered the grapes to anyone who wanted to care for them, then filled a Toyota Tacoma with grapes in late September without having sprayed a thing. Pedicini, a bartender who once owned a winery in Oregon, and the crew are fermenting them with wild yeast in a backyard barn in the Old North End. If all works out, they'll bottle about 25 cases in May, just to share with friends.

[pause] Report says land bank could help ease Vermont housing crisis; funding questions remain.

"Land banking is a very strong, large tool in the toolbox of actively combating blight, affordability, and the housing crisis," Nicole Justice Green said, per WCAX.

A state housing department report sent to legislators late last month weighs whether Vermont should create a land bank, an entity that takes on abandoned or tax troubled properties and can help get them back on the market at affordable prices. Commissioner Alex Farrell says the agency isn't directly proposing one, mostly because of outstanding questions like how to fund it, and Green, whose coalition operates 2 land banks, says a solid funding source is the biggest key. Vermont and New Hampshire are the only New England states without one.

[pause] South Burlington, Rutland are litmus tests for Act 250 exemptions.

"A lot of what Act 250 currently does and administers is a duplication of what happens at the local level," Paul Conner said, per The Other Paper.

South Burlington and Rutland City are the only 2 municipalities so far to start the process for Tier 1A status under the 2024 land use reform, which would let the city permit developments involving things like waste disposal or wetlands without sending the project through the same review process with the state. City Center and the Shelburne Road stretch between Swift Street and IDX Drive are expected to gain full exemptions, but the state's Land Use Review Board questioned whether the city's wider planned growth areas, especially east of Spear Street, truly reflect compact settlement. Board member Alex Weinhagen says his board is "learning by doing" with the first few pre-applications.

[pause] Study finds it's rare for big investors to swoop in to buy Vermont homes.

"We are not seeing any significant buying either by private equity, outside corporations, or even individuals here in Vermont," Alex Farrell said, per Vermont Public.

The state housing department's study found just 14 high frequency buyers, companies or people involved in 3 or more purchases in a given year, among nearly 30,000 single family home and duplex sales from 2022 through 2025, and together they made only 108 acquisitions. Newport's Tanguay Homes bought 43 of them, usually reselling within a year at an average gain of about $110,000, and owner Tony Tanguay says he buys homes with problems like faulty wiring that can deter a bank. Rep. Emilie Krasnow of South Burlington calls the findings good news but plans to reintroduce a bill reining in investor purchases come January.

[pause] Lake Champlain Chocolates factory staff vote down unionization effort.

"I’m hopeful that this is an eye-opening experience, and I’m hoping that the company really does rise to the occasion," Nic Smith said, per VTDigger.

Workers at Lake Champlain Chocolates' Williston manufacturing facility voted 20 to 12 against Teamsters representation last Tuesday, after organizers raised concerns about healthcare coverage and the rising cost of living and president Eric Lampman argued a union would change the culture of the 43 year old family owned business. The day before, a proposed 8 person unit of cashiers, baristas and others at the Church Street store filed its own petition to unionize, and the Teamsters say that vote will take place separately.

[pause] Vermont student proficiency in English, math rises for first time since pandemic.

"This follows a long period of declining and stagnant performance. So it is encouraging," Zoie Saunders said, per WCAX.

The Agency of Education's annual report card shows 56% of Vermont students proficient in English language arts and 42% in math in the 2025-2026 report, up from 48% and 33% in 2021, and chronic absenteeism fell to 20% from 25% in 2023-24. Saunders, the education secretary, says the achievement gap is widening, though, with students from economically disadvantaged communities and students with disabilities falling behind in both subjects, according to the agency.

[pause] Neighbors as community: Cohousing expands across Vermont.

"It’s a dynamic way to live. So that includes all the ‘Oh my goodnesses’ and the pleasant surprises," Sharyl Green said, per VTDigger.

Burlington Cohousing East Village is home to 56 neighbors who own their own homes but share a kitchen, green space, solar powered common spaces and dinner every other night, and it's one of 12 cohousing communities in Vermont, up from just 1 in 1995. Cost is one of the biggest criticisms of the model, but some East Village homes are designated as affordable housing and are listed and sold by Champlain Housing Trust. Vermont ranks seventh in the number of cohousing communities, more than many larger states.
