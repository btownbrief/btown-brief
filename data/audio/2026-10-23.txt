[pause] Burlington elementary students learn to swim through first-of-its-kind program.

"I didn’t need an adult today to swim and I’m not scared of deep water anymore," per WCAX.

For the first time this year, Burlington fourth graders are taking swim lessons at the YMCA during school hours, twice a week for 90 minutes, in what school officials say is the first program of its kind in Vermont. All six elementary schools come for three weeks as part of the YMCA's nationwide Safety Around Water Program, learning water safety, survival skills and even how to save someone in need. The district wants to make the deep end swim test a graduation requirement eventually.

[pause] As Beta Technologies Grows, ‘Betaville’ Sprouts in South Burlington.

"I can’t see how our property values are not going to diminish because of this massive project, particularly when Beta owns so many houses on our street," per Seven Days.

Beta has spent about $22.6 million on at least 13 properties around its base at Burlington International Airport over the past year, and a master plan before South Burlington's Development Review Board would more than double its footprint there to 100 acres over the next decade. Fueled by the $1 billion it raised going public last November, the company plans to add about 1,000 local employees by the middle of next year, and it paid $8 million in 2025 for Pete's RV Center, a parcel appraised at $2.2 million in 2021. Shunpike Road resident Dani Fisette says a quarter of the homes on her street became transitional rental housing for Beta workers earlier this year, and Beta says it can't commit to long term leases there. The plan could be approved within the next few months.

[pause] 5 takeaways from Phil Scott and Amanda Janoo’s first debate of the campaign.

"We can’t solve our problems with the same thinking that created them," per VTDigger.

Gov. Phil Scott and his Democratic and Progressive challenger Amanda Janoo met Wednesday in a friendly first debate hosted by Vermont Public, where Scott leaned on his record through the pandemic and the 2023 and 2024 floods and promised to propose tax cuts or credits, while Janoo, an economist running her first campaign, pitched raising taxes on the wealthiest Vermonters to pay for free primary care. She cited modeling showing surcharges on income over $250,000 could raise $114 million to $344 million a year but didn't directly answer what she'd do if that wasn't enough, and Scott didn't directly say what his tax package would include. They also split on nuclear power, with Scott backing a small scale plant, and on remote work for state employees. WCAX hosts another debate on Wednesday, Oct. 28 at 7.

[pause] A Colchester Tech Firm Has Been Leaning Into AI. It’s Laying Off 66 People.

"They kind of just shut me out, and now I feel like a criminal," per Seven Days.

Vermont Information Processing, a Colchester software company that had employed about 600 people in the state, is laying off 66, a number that came out through the state Department of Labor's database rather than from the company. VIP held an "AI Week" in April and last month bought Encompass Technologies, an AI focused beverage industry software firm in Fort Collins, Colo., to "accelerate AI innovation." One laid off worker says her virtual meeting lasted about 10 minutes and her access credentials were revoked minutes later, though those cut stay fully paid with health benefits through December.

[pause] ICE looks to boost staffing at intelligence hubs in Vermont.

"Earlier this month, protesters wrote anti-ICE messages with chalk on the facade of the Law Enforcement Support Center and blocked the road in front of the building using garden beds," per VTDigger.

A plan that isn't final calls for 24 contractor positions split evenly between ICE's Law Enforcement Support Center on Harvest Lane and its National Criminal Analysis and Targeting Center on Industrial Avenue, both in Williston, as part of a more than $100 million nationwide intelligence push for the branch that carries out arrests and deportations. Some of the workers would need top secret clearance, a final contract could be awarded around February.

[pause] Electricity Costs Are Soaring in Vermont.

"Nothing goes down in price, and some go up exorbitantly," per Seven Days.

Vermont's 17 utilities have asked regulators to raise rates 90 times since 2010, more than half of those in the past four years, and the average monthly residential bill has climbed 39% since 2019, from $97.25 to $135. Someone using the same amount of power as in 2019 now pays about $342 more a year, and the Department of Public Service predicts statewide rates will rise another 25% by 2030. Regulators approved a 5.5% Green Mountain Power increase in late August, below the 7.5% it asked for, and approved only $113 million of its $341 million in grid upgrades. Some places are seeing steeper jumps, with Hyde Park paying 35% more this year, Swanton seeking 30% after its Highgate Falls dam bladder tore and Jacksonville up 55% over three years.

[pause] Scott and legislative leaders draft emergency aid plan for home heating.

"We also think that people shouldn’t be freezing in their houses in the winter, so it seems a very appropriate use of this money," per Vermont Public.

With heating oil at $6 a gallon and No. 2 heating oil up nearly 60% in a year, Gov. Phil Scott and Democratic lawmakers are negotiating state money for the 15,000 Vermonters who rely on the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program, whose federal block grant is flat at $23 million. Keeping out of pocket costs at last year's level could top $8 million, drawn from a $50 million reserve set up to backfill federal cuts. The Emergency Board is scheduled to vote on a package today, and income eligible Vermonters can still apply through the Department for Children and Families' online benefits portal.

[pause] Essex failed to warn voters about a tax vote. Now the November result might not count.

"This was an honest mistake made by our town," per VTDigger.

Essex's November ballot has three questions on 1% local option taxes for meals and alcohol, rooms and sales, with half the money going to help lower property taxes, but voters never got the official warning that state law requires 30 to 40 days before the election. On legal advice the vote goes ahead as non binding, so any question that passes needs a second vote at Town Meeting on March 2, while a no vote in November is final. A town FAQ using 2025 numbers puts the net revenue at $1,058,030 after the state's share, and former selectboard member Bruce Post says the mistake eroded his trust in town leadership.

[pause] Advocates for homeless Vermonters say new rules put 'process over people'

"We want to fix the problem of homelessness. We have the task of putting people into 700 rooms," per Vermont Public.

Lawmakers and advocates want the Scott administration to reverse the Department for Children and Families' new motel rules under Act 143, this year's homelessness law, saying they delay or block people from getting shelter, and some are asking a legislative oversight committee to reject them. Since Sept. 1, the 211 helpline no longer handles motel intake on nights and weekends, and a lengthy questionnaire scores each household's risk, with lower scoring households only referred when 50 to 100 rooms are available. Even so, the state's motel rooms in use rose from 390 in late August to 668 on Oct. 19, still under the 700 allowed by law.
